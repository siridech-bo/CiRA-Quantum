Using Δω× — a field guide

how the crossover tells you whether to run a single robust gate or GBC
← Validation tool Verifier

The validation tool reports one headline number: the crossover center-line error Δω× (e.g. ≈ 2π × 2.7 kHz for a typical trap). This guide explains what it is, how to use it at the bench, and how to measure the quantity you compare it against.

The one-line rule
Measure your center-line error Δω, then:
   Δω  <  Δω×     →  run the SINGLE robust gate   (GBC's 4× time isn't worth it)
   Δω  >  Δω×     →  turn ON GBC                   (its ε⁴ suppression now wins)

Δω× is set by how noisy your trap is; your Δω is set by how well your qubit frequency is calibrated. Compare the two.

1 · What Δω× is

Δω is the center-line error: the mismatch between the qubit transition frequency and the MS drive's center frequency — a common σ_z drift on both qubits (an asymmetric error in the red/blue-tone geometry). Physical sources:

SourceSpecies affectedTypical size
Magnetic-field drift (Zeeman shift)⁴⁰Ca⁺, ⁸⁸Sr⁺ optical qubits~0.1–5 kHz between calibrations
Laser / AOM center-frequency miscalibrationallset by your lock; residual ~0.1–1 kHz
AC-Stark shift (drive-induced)alldepends on power; often compensated

Δω× is the value of Δω at which the single robust gate and GBC give the same fidelity — the point where GBC's coherent-error suppression exactly pays for its longer gate time. It is a threshold, in ordinary frequency (a value like 2π × 2.7 kHz means the qubit is off center by 2.7 kHz).

Related: the dimensionless error is ε ≡ Δω·τ (the total unwanted rotation, in radians); at Δω×≈2π×2.7 kHz with τ≈20 µs, ε ≈ 0.35. See Appendix B of the manuscript for the ε²→ε⁴ scaling.

2 · The decision rule

One number tells you which pulse scheme to load:

  • Δω < Δω× → run the single robust gate. Your center-line error is small enough that GBC's extra 4× gate time (and its ~4× incoherent error) would cost more fidelity than the coherent error it removes.
  • Δω > Δω× → turn on GBC. Now the center-line error is large enough that the ε⁴ suppression outweighs the 4× time.

Depth stops at one. Nesting GBC further (k ≥ 2) never helps — the optimal compensation depth is always k* ∈ {0, 1}, one GBC or none (manuscript E4). So the rule is binary: single, or one GBC.

3 · How to measure your Δω

Δω is the residual center-line error you cannot calibrate away — the drift between recalibrations, not the one-time offset you already null.

  1. Ramsey experiment on the qubit — gives the qubit frequency; compare it to your programmed drive center. The difference is the instantaneous Δω.
  2. Track it over a run — repeat that Ramsey (or your B-field monitor) over the hours you actually operate. The RMS drift is the Δω you should compare to Δω×.
  3. Or bound it from the physics — e.g. from your measured magnetic-field stability × the qubit's Zeeman sensitivity.

Workflow: characterize trap noise (heating ṅ, T₂) → the tool gives Δω× for your trap; characterize center-line stability (Ramsey / B-field) → that's your Δω; compare.

4 · Is 2.7 kHz a "good" number?

It is right in the experimentally accessible range — which is the point. A well-shielded trap drifts ~0.1–1 kHz between calibrations (→ stay single); a noisy environment, poor shielding, or long gaps between recalibrations gives several kHz (→ switch to GBC). The threshold lands where the choice genuinely flips, so the map is actionable, not academic.

5 · It moves with your trap noise (∝√I)

Δω× is not universal — it scales as √Iincoh, so it tracks your incoherent-error floor:

  • Better trap (lower heating / longer T₂) → lower Δω× → GBC pays off sooner.
  • Noisier trap → higher Δω× → you tolerate more center-line drift before GBC helps.

The 2.7 kHz figure is for one example (ṅ ≈ 1 phonon/ms, δ = 50 kHz). The validation tool's recommendation card recomputes Δω× live from your ṅ, T₂, and n̄ — so read it off there for your actual conditions, not from this page.

6 · Validate it on your hardware

You can also use Δω× to test the theory — this is experiment #1 in the tool:

  1. Inject a known center-line error Δω: step the magnetic-field offset (Ca⁺/Sr⁺) or the laser/AOM center frequency by calibrated amounts.
  2. Measure gate fidelity vs Δω for both schemes (single and GBC).
  3. Find where the two curves cross — that measured crossover should match the predicted Δω×.

Paste your F-vs-Δω data into the tool's "#1 Crossover verification" panel: it overlays the theory, extracts your experimental crossover, reports the agreement (χ²/dof), and exports a publication-ready figure — without altering your data.

7 · Worked example

Trap:   δ = 50 kHz gate  →  τ = 2π/δ ≈ 20 µs
        heating ṅ ≈ 1 phonon/ms,  n̄ ≈ 1
Tool:   Δω× ≈ 0.055 δ  =  2π × 2.7 kHz

Your qubit (from Ramsey over a 2-hour run):
   center-line RMS drift  ≈  2π × 1.2 kHz   →  BELOW 2.7 kHz  →  run SINGLE robust gate

Later, B-field shielding degrades:
   center-line RMS drift  ≈  2π × 4.0 kHz   →  ABOVE 2.7 kHz  →  switch ON GBC

In one sentence: measure how far your qubit center-line drifts (Ramsey / B-field); if it stays under Δω×, run the plain robust gate; if it exceeds Δω×, switch on GBC — and Δω× itself is set by how noisy your trap is, which the tool computes for you.
