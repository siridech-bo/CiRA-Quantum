## Abstract

Quantum reservoir computing (QRC) exploits the natural, uncontrolled dynamics of a
quantum system as a temporal feature map, training only a linear readout. Two
components usually left fixed — the *readout basis* and the *input encoding* — are
in fact the most accessible design levers on a real device. We study both on a
weak-coupling nuclear-magnetic-resonance (NMR) spin reservoir simulated with a
Lindblad master equation. First, we introduce a **physics-informed reduced
free-induction-decay (FID) readout**: because the reservoir Hamiltonian is diagonal
in the computational basis, its transverse-magnetization signal oscillates at a
*finite, analytic* set of single-quantum transition frequencies. Selecting and
merging these lines at the decoherence linewidth yields a compact,
device-independent spectral basis of dimension `D_eff` (e.g. `D_eff ≈ 100` for the
nine-spin ¹³C crotonic-acid system, versus the 653-peak readout used previously),
read by a fixed differentiable projection. Second, we make the *input encoding*
trainable by backpropagating through the differentiable reservoir (a "quantum
neural ODE"), while keeping the closed-form ridge readout. On the physics-informed
readout, a learned per-spin encoding beats the standard `arcsin(√s)` encoding by up
to ~19× (NARMA-2), ~2.6× (NARMA-10), and ~8× (Mackey-Glass) in test NMSE, confirmed
across seeds. A τ→0 ablation that removes the reservoir's cross-step memory
collapses the advantage to a mean-predictor, establishing that the gain is
**carried by the quantum reservoir's memory**, not by classical pre-processing. We
discuss the decoherence-limited regime in which these gains operate and the
hardware realizability of the trained encoding via the parameter-shift rule.

## I. Introduction

Reservoir computing (RC) trains only a linear readout on the state of a fixed,
randomly-initialized dynamical system, avoiding the cost and instability of training
the recurrent core. Quantum reservoir computing (QRC) replaces the classical
reservoir with a quantum system whose exponentially large state space and natural
nonlinearity promise a rich feature map at low training cost [Fujii & Nakajima 2017;
Nakajima *et al.* 2019]. NMR spin ensembles are a natural QRC substrate: they provide
many coupled qubits, well-characterized Hamiltonians, and an intrinsic
time-multiplexed readout — the free-induction decay (FID) [Hou *et al.* 2026].

Most QRC studies treat the reservoir as a black box and optimize only the readout.
Two design choices, however, are both physically accessible and largely unexplored:

1. **The readout basis.** The de-facto standard reads a large number of FFT peaks
   of the FID (e.g. 653). We show this *oversamples* the reservoir's actual spectral
   content and give a principled, minimal alternative.
2. **The input encoding.** The map from a scalar input to an RF rotation angle is
   almost always fixed (`arcsin(√s)`). We ask whether *learning* it — by
   backpropagating through the quantum dynamics — improves performance, and whether
   any improvement is genuinely quantum.

This paper answers both. Our contributions are: (i) a physics-informed reduced-FID
readout derived analytically from the Hamiltonian and made differentiable; (ii) a
trainable-encoding QRC ("regime B") with a leakage-free training protocol and
rigorous controls; (iii) multi-seed, ablation-confirmed evidence that a learned
encoding delivers a *quantum-mediated* performance gain on this substrate; and (iv)
an honest characterization of the regime and limits (decoherence, classical
baselines).

## II. Model

**Reservoir.** We simulate an `n`-spin weak-coupling (Ising-`ZZ`) NMR network,

```
H/h = Σ_i ν_i I_z^i + Σ_{i<j} J_ij I_z^i I_z^j ,      I_z = σ_z/2,
```

with per-spin chemical shifts `ν_i` (Hz) and scalar couplings `J_ij` (Hz). All terms
commute, so entanglement is generated by `ZZ` evolution acting on transverse states
prepared by the input pulses. Dissipation is a `T₁/T₂` Lindblad model (physical
pure-dephasing form). Systems: an exact nine-spin ¹³C crotonic-acid model (Hou
*et al.* 2026, "Paper 4") and generic `n`-spin molecules for scaling.

**Input & encoding.** A scalar input `s ∈ [0,1]` is applied as a rotation
`R_x(θ(s))`. The standard encoding is `θ = arcsin(√s)`. It may be *global* (all
spins share `θ`) or *per-spin* (each spin its own, frequency-selective angle).

**Evolution & readout.** Each input step evolves the state for a free-evolution time
`τ`; observables (or the FID) are sampled at `V` virtual nodes within `τ`
(time multiplexing). The readout is a ridge-regularized linear map,
`W = (XᵀX + αI)⁻¹Xᵀy`, fit in closed form.

**Two training regimes (labeled on every result).**
- **(A) Standard QRC** — reservoir *and* encoding fixed; only `W` is trained. This is
  the canonical RC setting and our baseline.
- **(B) Learnable encoding** — the same closed-form `W`, *plus* a trained input
  encoding. We render one reservoir step differentiable (a dense exact node
  propagator `P = exp((τ/V)ℒ)` applied `V` times, `ℒ` the Liouvillian) so that
  gradients of the loss flow through the quantum evolution to the encoding
  parameters (autograd verified against finite differences to ~1e-9 in complex128).
  This is a "quantum neural ODE": the reservoir is a fixed differentiable layer and
  only the input map is learned.

## III. Physics-informed reduced-FID readout

The FID reads the transverse magnetization `M⁺(t) = Σ_k ⟨σ_x^k⟩(t) + i⟨σ_y^k⟩(t)`.
Because `H` is diagonal, `M⁺` is a sum over **single-quantum transitions**: flipping
readout spin `k` against a fixed configuration `z` of the other spins gives, to
first order in weak coupling,

```
f_k(z) = ν_k + ½ Σ_{j≠k} J_kj z_j ,      z_j ∈ {+1,−1}.
```

Each resonance `ν_k` is thus split into a `2^{n-1}` `J`-multiplet; the *entire*
spectral content of the FID is this finite line set. Every peak of the 653-peak
readout is one of these lines (or an FFT skirt of one).

**Selection.** We enumerate the analytic lines and merge any pair closer than the
decoherence linewidth `Δf = 1/(πT₂)` (physically unresolvable), yielding `D_eff`
resolvable lines at *known* frequencies. The encoding sets only the line
*amplitudes*; the frequencies are fixed by `H`, so the basis is a constant of the
reservoir — no reference run or peak-finding is required. We read the lines by a
fixed **direct-DFT projection** `X = f · Φ`, `Φ_{m,k} = exp(i2πf_k t_m)`, which is a
single matrix multiply and therefore differentiable — cheap enough to backpropagate
through, unlike the dense 2048-point FFT.

**Compression.** For the nine-spin crotonic system (proton readout), 1280 raw
transitions merge to `D_eff ≈ 100` — the 653-peak readout oversamples ~6.5×. For a
generic six-spin system, `D_eff ≈ 137` (rising to 161 at doubled coupling, as the
multiplets widen). The reduced readout is thus both smaller and physically
interpretable, and — crucially — its differentiability enables regime (B).

## IV. Trainable encoding: protocol and controls

The encoder is a small MLP `s → θ` (per-spin output for maximal, frequency-selective
expressivity), trained by Adam through the differentiable reservoir; the ridge
readout is refit in closed form at every step. Rigor:

- **Leakage-free three-way split.** Ridge is fit on *train*; the encoder is selected
  on *validation*; the reported number is *test*. The encoder never sees test.
- **Guardrails.** A configuration is rejected as under-powered if the arcsin baseline
  test NMSE ≳ 0.8 (≈ mean-predictor); a learned "win" is rejected as degenerate if
  the encoder's angle map is near-constant (std < 0.1).
- **τ→0 ablation (the decisive control).** Re-initializing the reservoir before every
  input step removes cross-step quantum memory. A scalar-per-step input carries no
  classical memory, so a genuine quantum-mediated gain *must* collapse under this
  ablation; a surviving gain would betray a classical shortcut.
- **Hardware link.** The gradients we backpropagate coincide (verified in
  simulation) with those measurable on a spectrometer via the parameter-shift rule,
  so the trained encoding is, in principle, learnable on the device itself.

## V. Results

All results use the physics-informed reduced-FID readout (regime labels shown),
six-spin reservoir with doubled coupling (`D_eff = 161`, 322 real features),
`T = 1500`, leakage-free split. Test NMSE (mean ± s.d. over seeds); lower is better.

| Task (seeds) | arcsin (A) | learned per-spin (B) | gain | seeds won | τ→0 ablation (B) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NARMA-2 (3) | 0.101 ± 0.006 | **0.0052 ± 0.0013** | **~19×** | 3/3 | 0.999 → mean-predictor |
| NARMA-10 (3) | 0.312 ± 0.013 | **0.120 ± 0.019** | **~2.6×** | 3/3 | 0.997 → mean-predictor |
| Mackey-Glass (2) | 0.00063 | **0.000076** | **~8×** | 2/2 | — (one-step; trivially easy) |

**Two findings.** (1) A learned per-spin encoding beats the fixed `arcsin`
encoding across every task and seed. The effect is largest on the
encoding-sensitive task (NARMA-2, whose difficulty is an input nonlinearity) and
smaller but present on the memory-bound task (NARMA-10), consistent with the
encoding being the accessible lever while long memory is set by the fixed reservoir.
(2) The τ→0 ablation collapses the learned model to a mean-predictor on both memory
tasks — the advantage is **carried by the quantum reservoir's memory**. This is, to
our knowledge, the first multi-seed, ablation-confirmed demonstration that a learned
encoding produces a *quantum-mediated* QRC gain on a physics-grounded readout.

## VI. Comparison to classical models

We compare the learned QRC against two classical baselines on the identical
leakage-free split and test-NMSE metric: a hyperparameter-tuned LSTM (sweep over
hidden ∈ {32,64,128,256}, layers ∈ {1,2}, lr, with cosine schedule, gradient
clipping, multi-init, val early-stopping) and a leaky Echo State Network (ESN,
reservoir-size sweep). **Table II** (run `classical-baseline-548dbea6`).

| Task | QRC-learned | LSTM (tuned) | ESN (N=1000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| NARMA-2 | 0.0052 | 0.0114 | **0.0046** |
| NARMA-10 | 0.120 | 0.234 | **0.029** |
| Mackey-Glass (h=1) | 0.00008 | 0.0003 | ~0 (trivial) |

**Findings, stated plainly.** (i) Tuning the LSTM removes most of the apparent QRC
advantage seen against an untuned baseline (e.g. NARMA-2 LSTM improves ~12×); the
learned QRC still betters the *tuned* LSTM by ~2× on both NARMA tasks. (ii) However,
a well-sized classical **ESN matches the QRC on NARMA-2 and outperforms it ~4× on
NARMA-10**. We therefore make **no claim of quantum advantage over classical
reservoirs** on these benchmarks; the learned encoding narrows but does not close
the gap. This is consistent with the decoherence-limited regime (Sec. VII): the
effective dimension the readout accesses is small, so the reservoir cannot exploit
its full Hilbert space. (iii) One-step Mackey-Glass is trivially solved by all
models and is not discriminative; a harder horizon (h=10) is the appropriate test —
the QRC evaluation at h=10 is a pending experiment and is not reported here to avoid
a horizon-mismatched comparison.

The contribution of this work is therefore **not** a quantum-beats-classical result,
but (a) a physics-informed, differentiable readout and (b) a *quantum-mediated*
improvement of the input encoding, established with multi-seed and ablation controls
(Sec. V). *Figure 4:* NMSE-vs-`n_train` learning curves (tuned LSTM, ESN) with the
QRC operating point overlaid.

## VII. Discussion

The learned-encoding gain is a genuine, controlled improvement to a quantum
reservoir, but it operates in a **decoherence-limited** regime: on this NMR
substrate the effective dimensionality accessed by the readout remains low, because
`T₂` collapses the state faster than the Ising dynamics populate the `2^n` space.
The encoding and readout are therefore the levers that pay off *now*, while a true
many-body quantum advantage would require entangling dynamics faster than
decoherence (long `T₂` × strong coupling). The physics-informed readout is a step
toward that regime-awareness: it exposes exactly the resolvable degrees of freedom
the device actually offers, and no more.

---

## VIII. Conclusion & outlook

We introduced a physics-informed, differentiable reduced-FID readout and a
trainable-encoding QRC, and showed — multi-seed and ablation-confirmed — that a
learned encoding yields a quantum-mediated performance gain on a nuclear-spin
reservoir. Next: the tuned classical baselines and learning curves (Sec. VI);
on-device encoding training via the parameter-shift rule; and pushing toward the
coherence-over-decoherence regime where the full Hilbert space contributes.
